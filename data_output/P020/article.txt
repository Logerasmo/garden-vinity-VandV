                  1. Introduction

                Thin-ﬁlm solar cells (TFSCs) with a cadmium-telluride (CdTe) photon-absorbing layer are the second most
             common solar cells in the global marketplace after crystalline-silicon (c-Si) solar cells [1]. Although CdTe
                TFSCs’ performance has improved signiﬁcantly in last two decades, their power-conversion efﬁciency (denoted
                by η) is still far below the Shockley–Queisser (SQ) limit. While η reaches 82% of the SQ limit of c-Si solar cells,
                η is still restricted to 68% of the SQ limit for CdTe TFSCs [2], the SQ limit for CdTe TFSCs being 32.7% [2].
                  The primary η-limiting factors for CdTe TFSCs are: (i) small charge-carrier lifetimes; (ii) low charge-carrier
                    densities [3]; and (iii) low open-circuit voltage Voc [3, 4] caused by radiative and non-radiative recombination
                    that occurs through band tails, defects, and lower-bandgap states causing sub-bandgap absorption [1]. Also,
                     interfacial recombination and the charge-carrier selectivity of the back-contact lower both Voc and η [4].
                Another limiting factor for large-scale production and reduction of the levelized cost of electricity (LCoE) with
              CdTe TFSCs is the scarcity of tellurium [5].
                  The incorporation of selenium (Se) into CdTe to create the ternary alloy CdTe1−ξSeξ (CdTeSe) [4, 6, 7] has
                been found to increase the short-circuit current density Jsc [8], passivate CdTe grains and grain boundaries [7],
                and drastically increase the minority carrier lifetime [4, 9]. Munshi et al [8] demonstrated polycrystalline
               CdTeSe/CdTe solar cells achieving a record 19.1% efﬁciency with Jsc = 28.4 mA cm−2. Shah et al [10]
                    investigated the role of CdTe in polycrystalline CdTeSe/CdTe graded bilayer photovoltaic devices, achieving

efﬁciencies over 19% by leveraging CdTe as an electron reﬂector. Incorporation of a higher-bandgap CdTe layer
behind a lower-bandgap CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer enhances Voc while maintaining Jsc. A bandgap
gradient near the front junction by incorporating oxygenated CdS and CdSe layers, forming a penternary Cd(O,
S,Se,Te) region was studied by Li et al [11]. The consequent reduction of nonradiative recombination at the front
interface increased η to 20.03%. Cai et al [12] fabricated a graded CdTeSe solar cell with maximum efﬁciency of
16.4% using pre-oxidized CdTeSe powder sources to suppress Te sublimation in oxygen-free environments,
thereby enabling higher Se incorporation via metastable hexagonal CdTeSe phases and thus demonstrating a
promising pathway for controlled Se alloying in CdTe photovoltaics.
   These studies indicate the critical role of bandgap grading in enhancing efﬁciency, but bandgap grading,
especially of the nonlinear variety, of the photon-absorbing layer has not been studied in any signiﬁcant detail.
Hence, a comprehensive study comprising different bandgap-grading proﬁles—including linear, nonlinear, and
piecewise homogeneous—is required to fully understand the impact of bandgap grading on the device
performance. In addition to research on increasing minority-carrier lifetimes and reducing defect density,
therefore, innovative designs based on thinning the photon-absorbing layer and optimal bandgap grading of
that layer are also required to realize efﬁcient CdTeSe solar cells and further reduce the LCoE.
  We have developed a coupled optoelectronic model comprising a photonic submodel and an electrical
submodel to predict the efﬁciency of graded-bandgap TFSCs [13, 14]. Using this model, we have predicted that
an optimal nonlinearly graded-bandgap photon-absorbing layer can signiﬁcantly enhance the efﬁciencies of
CIGS [13, 15, 16] and CZTSSe [17] TFSCs. A MATLABTM implementation of the model is available from
GitHub via https://github.com/nibj/SolarCellSimulator.git.
   In view of the commercial signiﬁcance of CdTe TFSCs and the promise of bandgap grading, we report here
the results of our optoelectronic simulations of TFSCs with a graded-bandgap CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer
in order to determine the optimal bandgap-grading proﬁle and thickness of that layer. The thickness was varied
from 200 nm to 3000 nm. The bandgap was taken to vary either linearly, nonlinearly, or in a piecewise
homogeneous way in the thickness direction. Since CdTeSe transitions from the zincblende phase to the
wurtzite phase when the compositional parameter ξ exceeds 0.4 [18] and the wurtzite phase is currently held to
be unsuitable for photovoltaic applications due to low photoactivity [19], we initially optimized for ξ  0.4 and
then for ξ  1. Interfacial and grain-boundary recombination were ignored in favor of bulk recombination in
our study.
   This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the optoelectronic simulation of TFSCs in brief.
Numerical results are presented and discussed in section 3. Section 3.1 validates the model-predicted efﬁciency
of a CdTeSe TFSC with a homogeneous, 3000-nm-thick photon-absorbing layer against available experimental
data. Section 3.2 presents the results for the zincblende phase of CdTeSe and is divided into four subsections.
Section 3.2.1 provides optimal results for TFSCs with a homogeneous CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer and
section 3.2.2 for TFSCs with a linearly graded CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer. Optimal results for TFSCs with a
nonlinearly graded-bandgap CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer are discussed in section 3.2.3. Section 3.2.4
provides the optimal results for TFSCs with a piecewise-homogeneous graded CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer.
In order to spur technological developments to enlarge photovoltaic applications to span ξ ä [0, 1], section 3.3
provides model predictions for that range of the compositional parameter ξ. The paper ends with some remarks
in section 4.

2.1. Coupled optoelectronic model and optimization
In the coupled optoelectronic model, the photonic submodel implements the standard transfer-matrix method
[21] to determine the photon-absorption rate as a function of z everywhere inside the TFSC, assuming normal
illumination by unpolarized polychromatic light endowed with the AM1.5G solar spectrum [22]. The
formulation of the coupled optoelectronic model can be found elsewhere [23], together with numerical

   The real and imaginary parts of the relative permittivity εr(λ0) = ε(λ0)/ε0 were calculated for CdTeSe as

calculations are directly taken from experimentally reported studies and displayed in ﬁgure S3 of
Supplementary Information.
   With the photon-absorption rate taken to equal the electron-hole-pair (EHP) generation rate G(z) in the
semiconductor layers, an external voltage Vext applied across the contacts drives the electric current in the TFSC
[31]. The FTO/ITO/CdS/CdTeSe region (Lw < z < Ld in ﬁgure 1) was incorporated in the electrical submodel,
as all four layers contribute to charge-carrier generation. The bulk recombination mechanisms—radiative and
Shockley–Read–Hall—were incorporated into the electrical submodel. Auger recombination in CdTe is
generally considered negligible at typical doping densities below 1018 cm−3 [32] and was therefore neglected in
this study. Although interfacial defects can play an important role, they are considered negligible compared to
bulk defects [32], and were therefore omitted from this analysis.
   The electrical parameters used for all four semiconductor layers [20, 33–36] are provided in table S1 of
Supplementary Information. The electron afﬁnity of CdSe varies in the literature, with reported values ranging
from approximately 3.9 eV to 5.1 eV [33–36]. This variability arises due to differences in crystal structure (zinc
blende versus wurtzite), surface orientation, doping levels, and fabrication methods. To comply with the
ionization potential theory–which states that the ionization potential of CdSe should be higher than that of
CdTe–we adopted a value of 4.56 eV [35] for the electron afﬁnity of CdSe in all simulations.
   The drift-diffusion equations governing the number densities and ﬂuxes of electrons and holes, together
with the dc electric potential in the semiconductor layers are described in detail by Anderson et al [23]. We used
boundary conditions at the top plane (z = Lw) and the bottom plane (z = Ld) of the semiconductor region
assuming ideal ohmic contacts, with the addition of an externally applied voltage Vext on one contact. We elected
to use quasi-Fermi potentials to represent the number densities of electrons and holes so as to ensure that both of
the latter quantities are always positive. The derivation of the resulting partial differential equations is provided
by Ahmad et al [13]. To develop a special staggered ﬁnite-difference method [37] for these partial differential
equations, we approximated the quasi-Fermi levels at the mid-point of each mesh interval, while the ﬂuxes were
approximated at the mesh nodes. To enforce numerical continuity of the quasi-Fermi potentials at mesh nodes,
we developed an approximate integral representation of the quasi-Fermi potential away from the mid-point of a
mesh interval and used it to enforce continuity at the mesh nodes, effectively upwinding the equations. This
process is described in detail elsewhere [13]. The discretization of the ﬂux equations is based on standard central
ﬁnite differences and so the overall scheme is expected to have a second-order local truncation error [37].
   As a result, using Newton’s method [13] to solve the ﬁnite-difference equations, we can determine the
current density Jdev (by combining the predicted ﬂuxes of electrons and holes) and the power density P = JdevVext
as functions of the applied voltage Vext. The Jdev–Vext and the P--Vext curves yield the short-circuit current
density Jsc along with Voc and η.
   For completeness, a convergence study for the problem in this paper was performed by solving the drift-
diffusion model on increasingly reﬁned grids. The error in the computed efﬁciency with various mesh widths h

3. Results and discussion

3.1. Model validation
First, we validated our coupled optoelectronic model by simulating a conventional MgF2/FTO /ITO/CdS/
CdTe/ZnTe/Au solar cell containing a 3000-nm-thick homogeneous CdTe photon-absorbing layer [20]. The
simulated values of Jsc, Voc, the form factor FF, and η for ξ = 0 (Eg = 1.5 eV) are presented in table 1, alongside
the corresponding experimental data [20]. Our model’s predictions are in reasonable agreement with the
experimental data, the differences being due very likely to variances between the optical and electrical properties
used in the model and those realized in practice.


3.2. Optimization for zincblende phase (ξ  0.4)
3.2.1. Homogeneous bandgap
We then carried out the optoelectronic optimization of TFSCs with a homogeneous CdTeSe photon-absorbing
layer. The parameter space for optimizing η was set up as: Ls ä [100, 3000] nm and Eg Î [1.4, 1.5] eV. The
maximum efﬁciency predicted is 19.72%, which was obtained with Ls = 3000 nm and Eg = 1.4 eV (ξ = 0.4).
The values of Jsc, Voc, and FF corresponding to this optimal design are 28.73 mA cm-2, 818 mV, and 83.93%,
respectively.
   In the literature, the champion CdTeSe TFSC fabricated was reported to deliver Jsc = 31.40 mA cm-2,
Voc = 899 mV, FF = 79.30%, and η = 22.4% [41]. Unfortunately, we are unable to validate our model against
this TFSC due to the unavailability of its exact design.


3.2.2. Linear bandgap grading
Next, we maximized η by linearly grading the bandgap energy of the CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer, using
either equation (2) or equation (3). The resulting optimal values of Jsc, Voc, FF, and η are summarized in table 2,
along with the associated parameters Eg,min , Eg,max , and A.
   Backward grading yielded a maximum efﬁciency of 19.72% with Eg,min = 1.4 eV, Eg,max = 1.5 eV, A = 0,
and Ls = 3000 nm. This result matches the efﬁciency reported in section 3.2.1 for the homogeneous-bandgap
case, with no change observed in the values of Jsc, Voc, and FF.
   With forward grading, the maximum efﬁciency predicted is 21.79% with Eg,min = 1.4 eV, Eg,max = 1.5 eV,
A = 1.0, and Ls = 3000 nm. This represents a 10.49% relative improvement over the 19.72% efﬁciency achieved
with a homogeneous photon-absorbing layer of the same thickness. The optimal design delivers Jsc = 28.52 mA
cm-2, Voc = 884 mV, and a ﬁll factor of 86.62%. While Jsc remains nearly identical to that for the homogeneous-
bandgap case, Voc shows a notable 8.07% increase.

3.2.3. Nonlinear bandgap grading
We went on to maximize η by nonlinearly grading the bandgap energy of the CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer,
using equation (4). The resulting optimal values of Jsc, Voc, FF, and η are summarized in table 2, along with those
of the associated parameters Eg,min , Eg,max , A, α, K, and ψ.
   An efﬁciency of 21.22% is achieved with the parameters Eg,min = 1.4 eV, Eg,max = 1.5 eV, A = 1.0, α = 1,
K = 1.5, and ψ = 0.6. This represents a 7.60% improvement over the maximum efﬁciency of 19.72% obtained
with a homogeneous CdTeSe layer of the same thickness. For this optimal design, the corresponding values of
Jsc, Voc, and FF are 27.99 mA cm−2, 876 mV, and 86.51%, respectively.
   The maximum efﬁciency achievable with a nonlinearly graded photon-absorbing layer is nearly the same as
that with a linearly graded photon-absorbing layer. While the short-circuit current density is marginally greater
for the linearly graded layer compared to the nonlinearly graded layer, the open-circuit voltage is slightly lower
for the linearly graded layer. We conclude that both linearly and nonlinearly graded CdTeSe photon-absorbing
layers result in notably higher efﬁciency than a homogeneous CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer.

homogeneous grading of the photon-absorbing layer, as has been shown for CIGS TFSCs [16].
   Hence, we implemented piecewise-homogeneous grading of the CdTeSe photon-absorbing layer. This layer
was divided into nsub sublayers, each of unknown thickness Lj and uniform bandgap energy Egj . The quantities
{Eg j , L j }nj =sub1 were determined by optimizing η subject to the constraints (i) Egj Î [1.4, 1.5] eV for j ä [1, nsub]
and (ii) å nj =sub1 L j  3000 nm.
   Optimal values of Jsc, Voc, FF, and η for nsub ä {1, 2, 3, 5, 7} are presented in table 4. The table also includes
values of Egj and Lj for the nsub sublayers.
   The highest efﬁciency is achieved with two sublayers (i.e., nsub = 2), requiring Eg1 = 1.4 eV, Eg2 = 1.5 eV,
L1 = 1250 nm, and L2 = 1750 nm. This design yields an efﬁciency of 22.13%, representing a relative
improvement of 12.22% compared to the baseline design with a single homogeneous photon-absorbing layer
(see table 1). Although Jsc remains nearly unchanged, Voc increases by 9.9%. Additionally, the predicted
efﬁciency surpasses those obtained for continuously graded bandgaps: 21.79% for linear and 21.22% for
nonlinear grading, as reported in table 3.
    Optoelectronic optimization indicates that piecewise-homogeneous bandgap grading outperforms both
linear and nonlinear bandgap grading of the photon-absorbing layer. A possible explanation for the lower
efﬁciency predicted with nonlinear bandgap grading, compared to piecewise-homogeneous grading, lies in the
spatial variation of the internal electric ﬁeld. In nonlinear grading, the continuous and smooth variation of the
bandgap can lead to non-uniform electric ﬁeld throughout the photon-absorbing layer. These non-uniformities
may introduce regions where charge carriers experience unfavorable band slopes, which can increase the EHP
recombination rate and hinder carrier-collection efﬁciency. In contrast, the piecewise-homogeneous grading
approach allows more precise control over the internal electric-ﬁeld distribution and band alignment,
facilitating improved charge transport and reducing recombination losses at critical interfaces. Additionally, the
sharp transitions between adjacent sublayers in piecewise-homogeneous grading can generate strong localized
electric ﬁelds that promote efﬁcient charge-carrier separation and extraction, thereby contributing to enhanced
device performance. Repins et al. [45] also highlighted that overly gradual grading can be detrimental to
efﬁciency and suggested abrupt bandgap changes as well as defect-passivation strategies to improve charge-
carrier collection and reduce recombination.

4. Concluding remarks

We performed detailed optoelectronic simulations of thin-ﬁlm solar cells with a photon-absorbing layer made
of CdTeSe to determine the effects of compositional grading (i.e., bandgap grading) and absorbing-layer
thickness on solar-cell performance. The bandgap energy of the CdTeSe layer was taken to vary either linearly, or
nonlinearly, or in a piecewise-uniform fashion in the thickness direction. The thickness of the CdTeSe layer was
constrained to not exceed 3000 nm.
   Our simulations predicted that linear and nonlinear grading can deliver an efﬁciency of 21.79% and 21.22%,
respectively, with 3000-nm-thick CdTeSe layers in the zincblende phase. With a 3000-nm-thick CdTeSe layer
comprising two homogeneous-bandgap sublayers, the maximum efﬁciency predicted is 22.13%. If the wurtzite
phase of CdTeSe is also included in the optimization studies, the maximum efﬁciency attainable rises to 24.68%
with linear grading. We hope that these ﬁndings will be valuable for experimentalists aiming to enhance the
design of high-efﬁciency CdTeSe TFSCs.
   While our study was focused on bandgap grading in CdTeSe TFSCs under the assumption of no grain
boundaries, future work could explore detailed modeling of grain-boundary effects and the underlying defect
mechanisms in CdTeSe TFSCs. It is important to note that the enhanced efﬁciency achieved through bandgap
grading comes with increased fabrication complexity. The selection of a speciﬁc bandgap-grading approach
depends on balancing the possible efﬁciency gains with the associated fabrication costs. The exact nature of
efﬁciency verses fabrication cost can be quantiﬁed by our fellow experimentalists.
